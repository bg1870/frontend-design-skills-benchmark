It applies to marketing pages, dashboards, pricing pages, clickable prototypes, and additions to existing sites. It should not be loaded for backend-only work, nonvisual logic changes, prose-only deliverables, or analysis that does not involve implementing an interface.

# Design from the material, not the template

## Scope
Apply when implementing a browser-visible artifact or changing its visual presentation.
Outside this scope, do not impose this skill's workflow or output format.

## Result contract
Return the requested implementation in the task's delivery format, retaining any existing stack; do not substitute a design rationale or extra documentation, or migrate frameworks for styling.
Success requires a composition led by the actual content or task, visibly differentiated information levels, and controls that work within their stated scope. Exclude invented credibility claims, filler sections, and ornamental mock interfaces unrelated to the task.
Treat explicit requirements, supplied visual references, established site conventions, factual accuracy, and usable interactions as mandatory. Treat the numerical ranges and unrequested style choices below as defaults, not reasons to redesign an existing system.

## Decision rules
- R1. When extending an existing interface, inspect its adjacent screens and reuse its type roles, spacing, colors, borders, radii, and control behavior. Make the new work belong beside them; do not introduce a second visual identity to demonstrate creativity. When the brief explicitly requests a redesign, preserve required behavior rather than obsolete styling.
- R2. Always identify the primary user action and the material needed to make that action intelligible: an object, offer, record, result, or claim with evidence. Give that material priority in the initial viewport. For a working tool, show the working surface rather than a marketing introduction. For a promotional page, show what is being offered rather than only an abstract promise.
  - Include a section only if it supplies evidence, resolves a decision, or enables an action. Do not add testimonial, logo, metric, FAQ, or feature sections merely to fill a familiar landing-page sequence.
  - Write labels and copy using the supplied subject's nouns and actions. Replace “Elevate your experience” with the actual outcome; replace “Learn more” with a destination-specific label when the destination is known. Do not present invented customers, awards, prices, or performance claims as facts; sample content follows the fallback below.
- R3. When choosing a new composition, let the dominant material determine its structure instead of starting with a centered hero, two buttons, and three equal cards. Use these defaults only where the brief and existing system leave the choice open:
  - If appearance or physical material is the evidence, make a relevant image the largest visual field. Crop around its subject and place text in actual negative space or in an adjacent field, not over a busy subject with a compensating shadow.
  - If reading or explanation is the task, lead with a typographic hierarchy, a constrained reading column, and supporting figures or marginal information. Do not enclose each paragraph in a card.
  - If scanning or operating records is the task, lead with aligned rows, a useful initial selection or summary, and nearby controls. Keep secondary chrome quieter than the data; do not spend the first screen on an oversized welcome message.
  - If comparing offers is the task, align shared attributes and price units so differences can be scanned horizontally. Do not create equal-looking tiles whose differently ordered contents conceal the comparison.
  - For mixed pages, choose the first structure from the primary action in R2; let later sections change structure when their material changes. Do not repeat the hero geometry for every section.
- R4. When creating a visual identity, commit to a surface palette, a display/body type relationship, and a recurring treatment derived from available content. Encode them as shared style values before expanding the page. A catalog might reuse accession-style labels; a material study might reuse a distinctive crop; an operations tool might use status rails. Do not sprinkle unrelated gradients, blobs, glass panels, or textures as interchangeable decoration.
  - If the brief supplies no stylistic cues, use R3's content structure to choose the identity: image-led work takes its accent from the image; reading-led work puts contrast into type size and weight; record-led work reserves vivid color for actions and states. Do not default every case to the same fashionable color/font pairing.
- R5. Always establish shared alignment lines for the title, body, controls, and repeated content. Use a spacing scale rather than unrelated per-element margins; default to 4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, 48, 64, and 96px. Keep within-group gaps smaller than between-group gaps so proximity communicates membership without extra boxes.
  - When a wide viewport creates unused space, enlarge the evidence, improve comparison, or leave a deliberate margin. Do not stretch prose or scatter controls to fill it. Use unequal columns only when the main content and supporting content have unequal roles, not as arbitrary asymmetry.
  - When the opening is expressive, give one element clear scale dominance: the headline, image, or working artifact. Keep navigation, secondary actions, and supporting copy subordinate; do not make all four compete at display size.
- R6. Always assign typography by role before styling individual elements. Default to no more than two families, with an explicit fallback stack; use one when contrast in weight, size, and width already distinguishes the roles. Use a display face only for short display text, not dense labels or tables. Reserve tracked capitals for short metadata, not sentence-length subheads.
  - For new designs, start prose at 16–18px with 1.45–1.65 line height and a 45–75ch measure. Start dense interface text at 14–16px with 1.3–1.5 line height. Do not shrink body copy to rescue a crowded layout.
  - When a headline leads a promotional composition, start at 48–88px on wide screens and 32–48px on narrow screens, with 0.95–1.1 line height. For a task-oriented screen, start its title at 24–36px so records remain visible. Let content and supplied typography override these ranges when necessary.
  - When text wraps awkwardly, adjust its container width or responsive type size before inserting line breaks. Avoid a lone word dangling beneath a large headline; preserve natural wrapping under resizing. Use tabular numerals and consistent precision for compared numbers, not manually inserted spaces.
- R7. Always give colors roles: canvas, surface, primary text, secondary text, boundary, action, and semantic state. Reuse role values rather than selecting new grays and accent shades per component. Default to one action accent plus semantic colors; a multicolor identity must have an assigned use for each extra color.
  - When everything competes through saturation, retain saturation on the primary action or principal evidence and lower it elsewhere. Dark, light, warm, and cool canvases are all allowed; an unrelated dark hero followed by a white page is not a substitute for a content-led transition.
  - When using a gradient, shadow, or blur, give it a job: conveying a supplied identity, separating overlapping layers, or protecting legibility. Otherwise use a solid surface, spacing, or a rule. Do not decorate an empty region to hide missing content.
- R8. When elements belong to one continuous collection, use shared alignment, spacing, or separators before individual cards. Use a bounded surface when it identifies an independently actionable item, a separate state, or an actual layer. Do not wrap every heading and its paragraph in a rounded rectangle.
  - When a container is justified, avoid redundant separation: start with a fill, a border, or a shadow, not all three. Reserve stronger elevation for overlays and floating controls. Match nested corner radii to their inset so inner corners do not bulge beyond outer ones.
- R9. When meaningful imagery is available, use it at a resolution and crop that reveal the relevant subject. Reserve its dimensions to prevent layout jumps and supply a fallback treatment. Inspect the crop at both wide and narrow sizes; a face, product detail, or embedded label must not disappear behind text or off the edge.
  - When illustrating a product, show a relevant workflow or representative object with legible labels, not a miniature generic dashboard full of unreadable rectangles. Use one consistent icon language; do not mix emoji, text glyphs, and unrelated icon styles as decoration.
- R10. When rendering supplied records, preserve their values and relationships; do not alter them for visual variety. When creating labeled sample content, vary lengths and states enough to expose wrapping, outliers, and empty-state behavior rather than repeating identical placeholders. Keep units, time ranges, denominators, and status meanings adjacent to the values they qualify. Charts must encode those values consistently, not use decorative bar lengths or invented trends.
- R11. Always connect visible controls to an observable result: navigation reaches its destination, a filter changes the visible records, a disclosure opens, or a form validates and acknowledges its actual outcome. Do not use inert buttons, empty links, or success messages for operations that did not happen.
  - When implementing a stateful control, include its relevant selected, focus, disabled, error, and pending states in the same visual system. Keep button dimensions stable across labels and loading states. Use visible labels for ambiguous icons and distinguish the primary action from secondary actions by treatment, not just position.
- R12. When columns or controls no longer fit their content, preserve task order and content before desktop geometry. Collapse supporting columns below the main content; reduce peripheral margins before type size. Reflow navigation into an operable control rather than shrinking its labels. Keep actions near the content they affect.
  - When a comparison or data table cannot fit, retain its labels and relationships in a clearly scrollable region or labeled stacked records. Never clip values, hide essential columns without access, or introduce whole-page horizontal scrolling. Avoid fixed heights on text-bearing containers.
- R13. Always use native links, buttons, inputs, and headings for their corresponding roles. Supply input labels, visible keyboard focus, descriptive alternatives for informative images, and empty alternatives for decorative images; ensure hover-only content is also reachable by focus or activation. Keep normal text contrast at least 4.5:1 and large text at least 3:1; large means at least 24px regular or 18.7px bold. Keep boundaries needed to identify controls and focus indicators at least 3:1 against adjacent colors.
  - When a target is visually small, enlarge its hit area toward 44×44px without forcing the glyph itself to grow. Do not communicate errors, selection, or chart categories by color alone; add text, shape, or an indicator. Legibility and operability win over faint text and hairline minimalism.
- R14. When adding motion, tie it to a state change or spatial relationship. Default control transitions to 120–220ms using opacity or transform; avoid animating every property. Under reduced-motion preferences, remove nonessential movement. Do not hide initial content behind a reveal, delay access for an entrance sequence, or add perpetual motion to an otherwise static task.

## Limits and fallbacks
- If subject details are missing, choose the smallest coherent content set needed for the primary task without asking questions. Use clearly labeled sample data in prototypes; omit unsupported public-facing claims instead of fabricating proof. Represent unknown required values as unavailable, not as plausible facts.
- If no usable image or font asset is available, use a deliberate text-led layout, a locally available fallback font, or a simple inline diagram that explains the subject. Do not depend on an unprovided asset, broken image, external download, or random stock-photo substitute.
- If a backend or external action is unavailable, implement a local demonstration and label its scope at the action. Show “Saved in this demo” only after local state changes, not “Account created.” For actions that cannot be simulated honestly, disable them with a nearby reason while keeping the rest of the artifact usable.
- If requirements compete, explicit task constraints govern scope and reference fidelity. Within those constraints, truthful, readable, operable content wins over decoration; R1's inherited system then wins over R3–R8's defaults. Unless exact visual reproduction is requested, correct inherited styles that block reading or interaction rather than copying their defects. Relax decorative treatments, default size ranges, and optional sections first. If a required feature remains impossible, retain the usable portion and mark the unavailable feature in its interface; record implementation-only dependencies in code comments.
- If rendering or interaction inspection is unavailable, inspect the source for the checks below and make no claim of visual or runtime verification. Do not introduce tool or script dependencies merely to follow this skill.

## Before returning
- Check the initial viewport: can the user identify the offering or task, see relevant material, and find the next action without an explanatory introduction? If not, move the real content upward and remove competing ornament under R2.
- For a new promotional or exhibition surface, mentally replace the name with an unrelated product. If the imagery and every major section still fit unchanged, replace the most prominent generic section with subject-specific evidence or a subject-specific interaction under R2–R4; changing the accent color is not a repair.
- Check the rendered artifact when inspection is available at the requested viewport, a narrow width around 360px, and a wide width around 1440px. Inspect actual headline wraps, crops, crowded controls, unintended overflow, and the transition between sections. Repair structural failures before tuning decoration; keep the task's reference viewport authoritative for fidelity.
- Check repeated edges, label baselines, icon optical centering, inner padding, and number alignment. Correct shared tokens or component styles instead of accumulating one-off offsets. Remove any container whose deletion leaves grouping and interaction equally clear under R8.
- Check the primary flow, keyboard reachability, empty/error states, and text at 200% zoom. Repair dead controls and clipped content using R11–R13; if execution is unavailable, verify the corresponding handlers, semantic elements, state branches, and reflow rules in source without claiming they ran.
- Return when the requested artifact satisfies the result contract and these checks have no known unresolved defect. If a mandatory condition cannot be met, use the applicable fallback above rather than claiming success or continuing an unbounded redesign.

## Decision examples
- Normal case: Build a new ceramics shop with supplied product photos and prices; glaze detail drives purchase, and no site styling is supplied. Choose a large close crop of an actual pot, a subordinate title, and an open product collection with visible prices, not an abstract gradient hero and three generic benefit cards. R2–R3 make the material the visual lead; R8 does not require boxes around a continuous collection.
- Boundary case: The same ceramics shop must instead extend a supplied site with a compact catalog header and bordered product cards. Reuse that header and card system while choosing crops that show the glaze; do not replace the established opening with the formerly preferred large-image composition. The supplied system activates R1 and takes precedence over R3's new-composition default.
