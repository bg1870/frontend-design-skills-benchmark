name: beautiful-frontend
description: This skill guides the creation, redesign, or extension of browser-rendered marketing pages, dashboards, pricing surfaces, application screens, and clickable prototypes when the task includes visual decisions. It turns content priority into composition, typography, surfaces, and working interaction states while preserving established site conventions outside the requested change boundary. It should not be loaded for backend-only work, logic-only fixes with no visual change, non-browser artifacts, or exact reproductions that leave no discretionary visual choices.

# Content-led browser design

## 1. Scope and precedence
Scope: Shape browser pages and interactive screens whose hierarchy, layout, typography, media, or control states remain open to design. Correct undifferentiated emphasis, content-independent decoration, and layouts that work only with short placeholder text. Do not change product scope, invent capabilities, or redesign neighboring surfaces.
Apply this skill to browser-rendered work within that scope. Resolve visual conflicts in this order: content access and operable controls; explicit task requirements; established conventions in an existing surface; this skill's defaults.
In extensions, inherit the surrounding typography, spacing, colors, component behavior, and navigation unless the task explicitly changes them; apply this skill to unresolved choices.
Per artifact: identify whether the task creates, extends, or redesigns a surface, and identify the allowed change boundary from the supplied brief and material. Do not widen it to showcase the skill.

## 2. Perceptual priorities
Policy: First, task-bearing information outranks framing: the offer, working object, or decision evidence gets more visual weight than navigation, introductory labels, and decorative media. Second, relationships outrank component independence: comparable values share alignment and related content shares space before receiving separate boxes. When these priorities conflict with an attractive treatment, reduce oversized site chrome, repeated card enclosures, and atmospheric effects rather than shrinking the task-bearing content.
Use this priority order to resolve discretionary choices in later sections; do not introduce unrelated visual motifs merely to make regions look different.
Per artifact: assign the priorities to actual content or controls. Choose concrete visual values that express them; do not copy an imagined reference page.

## 3. Content and attention
Policy: For an unfamiliar offer, lead with the concrete benefit, follow immediately with evidence, then present the commitment. For an operational task, lead with the current object or consequential exception, then the information and action needed to proceed; omit a promotional introduction. A blocking error takes precedence in either case. Place qualifications beside the claim or action they qualify; move background explanation later. Allow only one highest-emphasis target in each active task region; supporting links and metadata must not share its treatment. Keep peer alternatives equally emphasized until selection, unless the brief supplies a defensible recommendation.
Per artifact: identify the visitor's main task, the information needed to complete it, and a ranked content order before arranging regions. Determine which items require reading, comparison, scanning, or action.
Use supplied content first. When content is missing, use plausible, explicitly illustrative material with realistic lengths and variation; do not invent endorsements, credentials, or factual performance claims.
Do not delete required content or shorten labels merely to fit a preferred composition. Omit sections that have no task or information to serve.

## 4. Composition and spatial rhythm
Policy: Establish shared content edges and explicit subcolumns; align headings, body starts, and repeated controls to these rather than positioning each block independently. Center a self-contained message or decision only when it has no repeated reading or comparison axis; otherwise align text to its reading start. In reading-led work, place dominant content first and give its introductory region more area than adjacent supporting items. In operational or comparison work, give the working region the largest uninterrupted span while keeping peer alternatives equal in geometry.
Give a region a separate container only when its boundary defines an independently selectable, movable, or navigable object, or separates children with a distinct shared owner or status; otherwise use a shared surface with spacing or separators. Do not turn every section or repeated statement into a card. Choose the strongest compositional gesture from the content: if decisive evidence is visual, use subject-led scale and crop; otherwise, if the task depends on repeated comparisons, use uninterrupted aligned fields; otherwise let the leading text's scale and placement carry the composition. Keep the other treatments subordinate.
Rhythm: Within-group gaps must be smaller than between-group gaps, which must be smaller than gaps between task sections; make each outer spacing tier at least 1.5 times the next inner tier. In dense rows, a shared boundary may replace the larger gap, but not both boundaries and spacious padding. Give sustained reading more line and paragraph breathing room than repeated comparison; keep comparison rows compact and regular, adding extra separation only at a category change. Never stretch rows just to match neighboring artwork.
Per artifact: choose columns, widths, spacing values, and component structure from the content order and available space. Keep repeated roles aligned and spaced consistently.
Use proximity, alignment, and shared boundaries to communicate actual relationships; do not add wrappers, empty panels, or ornamental sections to fill space.

## 5. Typography and text behavior
Policy: Use a display tier only for a narrative introduction; otherwise let the task heading lead without a billboard-sized title. Distinguish display through scale or an expressive face, not simultaneous outline, gradient, capitalization, and tracking effects. Section headings must exceed body text in size or weight; body text carries sustained reading; labels use the body family with tighter grouping; metadata sits below body emphasis without becoming illegible. Align comparable numbers by place value, use tabular figures there, and keep units adjacent; reserve monospaced text for code or identifiers rather than making all data look technical.
Permit one expressive type treatment per task region. Introduce a contrasting display face only when narrative is dominant and the available face supports the actual language and weights; otherwise use the body family across roles. When pairing, compare lowercase text at equal size: choose similar apparent x-height and contrasting letterform structure, keeping controls in the body face. If the candidate needs compressed tracking or synthetic weights to fit the long title, retain the body family. Use available faces before adding a font resource.
Text behavior: Bound sustained body reading to roughly 45–75 characters per line with unitless line height between 1.45 and 1.75; below that available width, use the full column rather than enforcing the minimum. Titles, action labels, validation messages, and values needed for a decision must wrap. Truncate only secondary identifiers in scan-heavy lists, and only when focus or an explicit detail control exposes the full selectable value; a hover tooltip alone is insufficient. Let multi-line buttons and labels increase their containers' height.
Per artifact: choose available fonts, role sizes, and measures; use actual long titles, labels, and representative values while determining layout.
Preserve selectable text, meaningful heading order, and readable content at twice the default text size. Never use tiny text, forced line breaks, or clipping to rescue a composition.

## 6. Color, surfaces, and visual assets
Surface policy: Use background for continuous reading or work, foreground for content, emphasis for the next action or selected object, and dedicated status roles for outcomes. Quiet supporting text through scale and weight before reducing contrast; require at least 4.5:1 for ordinary text and 3:1 for large text and necessary control boundaries. Do not reuse warning or failure treatments as idle decoration. Separate non-floating groups with either a boundary or a surface change, not an automatic stack of border, tint, and shadow; reserve shadows for actual overlap or elevation. Keep corner geometry consistent for equal roles and tighter at joined edges than standalone ones. Confine texture and gradients to identity-bearing introductions or meaningful visualizations; omit them elsewhere, especially behind dense reading or comparison.
Asset policy: Give media space only when it demonstrates the product, supplies evidence, establishes relevant human or physical context, or is itself the object being examined. If examining media is the task, it may dominate; otherwise it must not displace the decision evidence or next action. Icons earn space by identifying an action, category, or status, not by decorating every heading; match their optical weight to nearby text and keep one consistent construction across a set.
When suitable optional media is unavailable before implementation, remove its planned slot and return that space to text or data evidence. When media is required, provide an informative labeled fallback rather than unrelated stock imagery or an invented product screenshot. If an asset fails at runtime, retain its reserved bounds and expose a useful subject description or equivalent information, not a broken-image glyph or empty frame.
Per artifact: choose exact role colors, surface values, assets, crops, and aspect ratios. Keep repeated semantic roles consistent; keep decorative color distinct from status and action signals.
Pair color-coded meaning with text or shape; keep text distinguishable from its actual background in every state. Put essential meaning in text even when media fails.
Choose crops around the subject or informational feature; preserve image proportions and icon consistency. Do not replace unavailable meaningful media with unrelated decoration.

## 7. Adaptation under pressure
Policy: Within the region that stops fitting, respond in order: reduce discretionary outer and section space without flattening the grouping hierarchy; collapse supporting columns below the dominant region; move optional background detail into labeled disclosures while keeping prerequisites beside their controls and required supporting content in the reading flow; then replace navigation that still cannot fit with a named expandable menu. Keep current location and the main task action outside the collapsed navigation. Do not shrink text to preserve column count. For dense records or spatial content, reflow into labeled records when meaning survives removal of column alignment; otherwise preserve the table, timeline, or canvas in a locally scrollable region with an overflow cue. Pin row or axis identifiers only if this leaves room for a complete data value; otherwise let identifiers scroll with their records.
Per artifact: place layout transitions where content stops fitting or relationships become unclear; choose thresholds from that failure, not from a named device.
Preserve the main task and meaningful reading order across widths. Do not hide required actions or data to create a cleaner narrow view.
Contain unavoidable two-dimensional overflow within its own region and make access discoverable. Allow long content and enlarged text to grow without overlapping controls.

## 8. Interaction, states, and motion
Policy: Give the primary action the emphasis treatment; use neutral outlines or text for secondary actions. Unavailable actions retain readable labels, lose active emphasis, show the reason near the control, and reject activation without pressed feedback. Enabled controls change tone on hover without moving geometry and give immediate pressed feedback on activation. Focus uses a visible external indicator, while selection uses a persistent internal mark plus text or shape, so a selected item can also visibly hold focus. During pending work, keep the triggering control's footprint, identify the work in progress, and prevent duplicate submission. Errors name the failed operation and recovery next to their cause; completion leaves a stable confirmation or changed result rather than only a disappearing toast. Empty states name what belongs in the region and offer creation or filter reset when supported; otherwise explain the absence without a fake action. Announce asynchronous feedback without moving focus away from unfinished input.
Motion policy: Animate only changes whose spatial continuity would otherwise be lost, such as opening a panel or moving an item; keep such transitions within 100–240 milliseconds and do not animate surrounding content merely because it entered the viewport. Other state changes are immediate. With reduced motion, switch directly to the final arrangement and retain the same text and state indicators; no looping decorative movement in either mode.
Per artifact: map each visible control to an actual outcome and the states reachable in the requested scope. Include loading, empty, error, success, and disabled states wherever the behavior can produce them.
Give controls names, keyboard operation, and visible focus; do not make essential information available only on hover. Keep feedback near its cause and preserve entered data on recoverable errors.
Motion must not delay access to content or controls. Respect reduced-motion preferences. When a prototype simulates an outcome, make its scope clear rather than implying unavailable persistence or connectivity.

## 9. Implementation fidelity
Implement the requested scope in the supplied project conventions; reuse existing components and shared values before introducing equivalents.
Represent text and controls as browser content with appropriate semantics, not as a flattened picture. Express recurring visual decisions through shared values and reuse structure for repeated roles.
Reserve media space, supply font and asset fallbacks, and ensure essential content remains available when an enhancement fails. Do not introduce resources whose loading cost delays the main task without a task-specific need.
Per artifact: choose implementation details from the available environment and required behavior; add dependencies only for a concrete capability that the existing project cannot provide.

## 10. Completion gate
Rejection tests:
1. Composition fails if supporting panels compete with the dominant task region in area and emphasis, or peer alternatives have unequal comparison geometry. Reallocate span and space to the task, lower supporting emphasis, and equalize comparison peers.
2. Text hierarchy fails if labels or metadata match the leading heading's treatment, or section headings cannot be distinguished from body paragraphs. Restore role-specific size or weight, remove competing expressive treatments, and reflow long text rather than reducing it below the reading bounds.
3. Surfaces or media fail if shared-task rows look independently actionable solely because of isolated cards, or decoration dominates a task that is not media examination. Merge the rows onto a shared surface, remove redundant enclosure effects, and reduce or replace the competing media with task-bearing evidence.
Review the browser-rendered result at a wide and narrow width, with long content, enlarged text, missing media, and the applicable interaction states. Inspect the primary task using keyboard navigation.
Fix lost content, blocked actions, overlaps, clipping, unstable layout, and unreadable state distinctions first; then fix violations of the authored hierarchy and visual policies.
Stop when these failures and the authored rejection tests are resolved; do not invent more features or decoration as a finishing step.
If rendering or interaction cannot be inspected in the available environment, distinguish what was checked from what remains unverified; never claim a visual pass from source inspection alone.
